# Linguistic Foundations: EN↔JP

Why English-to-Japanese translation fails at the structural level. These aren't style preferences — they're features of how Japanese organizes information differently from English.

---

## Contrastive Rhetoric

**Reader-responsible vs writer-responsible prose (Hinds 1987).** English is writer-responsible: the writer spells out every connection, transition, and logical step. Japanese is reader-responsible: the reader infers connections, fills gaps, and interprets implicit meaning. Translated Japanese that is too explicit in its logical connections (しかし, したがって, さらに at every paragraph boundary) sounds like a textbook.

**Topic-comment structure.** English is subject-prominent (sentences need explicit subjects). Japanese is topic-prominent (sentences organize around a topic marked by は, and the rest comments on it). Once a topic is established, it drops away — Japanese drops the subject in ~74% of clauses where English requires one (Hinds 1982). Repeatedly marking the subject feels like the writer doesn't trust the reader.

**Ki-shō-ten-ketsu (起承転結).** Japanese rhetorical structure can be inductive — building context before arriving at the point — rather than English's thesis-first deductive approach. This doesn't mean Japanese copy must follow this pattern, but it explains why translated copy that leads with claims can feel aggressive. Modern Japanese business writing uses hybrid structures, but the impulse to ground the reader in context before asserting remains.

---

## Discourse Cohesion

**Clause chaining.** Japanese builds complex meaning through subordinate clauses chaining before a final verb. A single Japanese sentence can contain what English expresses as 3-4 sentences. English achieves complexity through coordination (and, but, or). Japanese achieves it through subordination (〜て, 〜し, 〜ので, 〜ながら, 〜が, 〜ため). The clause connector, not the period, is the natural rhythm break.

**Zero anaphora.** Dropping subjects, objects, and topics when recoverable from context isn't omission — it's the unmarked, natural form. Stating what's already known is marked (emphatic or contrastive). English copy that uses "you" and "we" repeatedly must be restructured so the sentence works without explicit subjects.

**Sparse inter-sentence connectives.** Where English uses overt discourse markers (however, therefore, moreover), native Japanese prose uses them sparingly, relying on implicit logical connection, clause chaining within sentences, and paragraph-level juxtaposition.

## Register in Commercial Copy

Japanese has a grammaticized politeness system (keigo) but register in commercial copy is not just about politeness levels. Key insight: **native Japanese documents shift register intentionally based on what each section is doing.**

- **Value propositions / descriptions:** です/ます base. Confident and declarative. Avoids ください — presenting, not commanding.
- **CTAs:** Softer forms — 〜してみませんか, まずは〜, 〜をご覧ください. Invitations, not commands.
- **Taglines:** Can be plain form. Bold, short.
- **Onboarding / instructional copy:** ください is appropriate — the reader has opted in and expects direction.
- **B2B / enterprise:** Higher keigo. ご検討いただければ幸いです rather than 検討してください.

Applying one register uniformly throughout a document is a translation marker. Real documents shift.

## Translationese Markers (翻訳調)

What native Japanese readers detect as "this was translated":

1. **Pronoun density** — too many 私/あなた/彼ら where zero anaphora is natural
2. **Sentence isolation** — ideas as discrete sentences rather than chained clauses
3. **Paragraph rhythm** — English-style short paragraphs instead of longer paragraphs with internal clause structure
4. **Over-explicit logical structure** — まず...次に...最後に where sequence would be implicit
5. **Register uniformity** — same politeness level throughout
6. **Abstract vocabulary** — nominalized concepts (生産性, 接続性) where verbs are more natural (生産的に働く, つながる)
7. **Passive voice calques** — English passives rendered as 〜される when active constructions are more natural

## Japanese Copywriting

Native Japanese commercial writing (distinct from translated copy):

- **Leads with context, not claims.** Grounds the reader before asserting.
- **Short phrases, long connective flow.** Individual phrases are concise but chain into longer sentences. Rhythm is phrase→phrase→phrase→conclusion, not sentence. sentence. sentence.
- **Concrete over abstract.** What actually happens, not what it conceptually represents.
- **Register shifts intentionally.** Different sections signal different relationships with the reader.
- **Leaves white space in meaning.** Stops short of the explicit conclusion, letting the reader complete the thought. Reader-responsible rhetoric applied to commercial writing.
- **Uses writing system strategically.** Kanji = density/authority. Hiragana = softness/warmth. Katakana = foreignness/modernity. English = global/aspirational. The mix within a line is a design decision.
